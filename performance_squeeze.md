# On CutMix

I'd expect it to be neutral at best, and there are three fairly specific reasons why this task is unusually hostile to it.

**The area/label proportionality assumption fails.** CutMix assigns λ from the pixel area of the pasted box. That is a reasonable proxy when the evidence is spread over the object (cat texture is cat texture everywhere). Here the age evidence is concentrated on a transect from nucleus to margin, and it is densest exactly at the margin where the last few increments are packed. A box covering 30% of the canvas might remove the mounting medium and nothing else, or it might remove the outermost three increments — the ones that separate age 8 from age 9. Same λ, wildly different label damage. You would be injecting label noise with a variance you can't control.

**A spliced otolith is not an intermediate otolith.** With a nominal label, a chimera plausibly contains evidence for both classes. With a counting task, splicing an age-4 section into an age-9 section produces a ring sequence that is discontinuous and corresponds to no fish. Radford's own note about CLIP failing at "counting near-identical objects" is the same underlying difficulty, and you cite it in the intro; mixing makes that harder, not easier.

**CORN doesn't take soft targets cleanly.** This is worth separating out, because I think there's an inaccuracy in §2.4 that matters here. Lines 300–302 say each of the K−1 tasks is supervised by the whole training set — that's CORAL's property. CORN's defining move is the *conditional* one: task k is trained on the subset with y ≥ k, which is how it gets P(y > k | y ≥ k) with per-task weights while staying rank-consistent. So the subsets are nested and shrinking, and the top thresholds (8→9, 9→10) are trained on only a few hundred specimens. Fractional labels don't have a well-defined home in a conditional subset scheme. It's also directly relevant below: your largest gains are at ages 8–10, which are precisely the thresholds with the least conditional supervision.

The one salvageable variant: **same-class or ±1-class CutMix**, where λ never enters the label. That removes the mixing problem entirely and keeps the regularising effect. I'd still rank it below the options below, since splicing still creates ring discontinuities, but it's the version worth one run if you want to say something about CutMix in the paper.

# What I'd actually try, roughly in order of expected return

**1. Anisotropic elastic warping.** This is the one I'd push hardest, because it's the only augmentation on the list that is genuinely label-preserving by construction and generates variation the model has never seen. Estimate the long axis of the section, then apply a thin-plate-spline or elastic deformation *orthogonal* to it while leaving the metric along the reading axis intact. Increment count along the transect is unchanged, so the label is exactly preserved, but you get plausible morphological variation of a kind that fixed affine transforms can't produce. If you want a version that also perturbs the reading axis, you can apply a smooth monotone warp along it and note that this simulates real variation in growth rate between fast- and slow-growing fish — but then you're perturbing increment spacing, which is the signal, so I'd keep it small or skip it.

**2. Crop-box jitter.** Your images are cropped from slides with 20+ otoliths, so the crop box comes from an upstream segmentation step whose variability the model currently never sees. Jitter the box by ±5–10% on each side during training. This is nearly free, is honestly motivated, and buys robustness to the deployment pipeline rather than just accuracy on your fixed crops. Related and equally cheap: you pad to square, so randomise the *placement* within the padded canvas instead of always centring. SigLIP2 uses learned position embeddings, so translation invariance is not free and this is a real augmentation rather than a no-op.

**3. Randomise the CLAHE parameters instead of fixing them.** You currently apply clip limit 3.0 with a scaled tile grid in a single deterministic pass. Sampling the clip limit (say 1.5–5) and the tile grid per training image makes the encoder invariant to the enhancement parameters rather than tuned to one setting, and it's a cleaner way to inject contrast variation than generic brightness jitter, because it perturbs local contrast the same way the acquisition does. Pair it with a low-frequency multiplicative illumination field to simulate uneven reflected light across the slide.

**4. Test-time augmentation over embeddings.** Your two-stage design makes this unusually cheap: extract embeddings under K augmented views (hflip, ±5° rotations, small translations), average them, then feed the averaged embedding to Ridge. No retraining, no change to any claim in the paper, and typically worth around a point. Same for averaging across your five existing seed adapters — the base encoder is shared, so five adapters is 40 MB, which sits nicely inside the modularity argument you're already making in §3.8. Moen et al. (2023) report 72.7% single model against 78.6% with a six-model ensemble, so there's precedent in exactly this literature.

**5. Targeted oversampling at the sparse ages, with the stronger augmentations applied there.** Given the conditional-subset point above, the top CORN thresholds are the data-starved part of the model, and ages 8–10 are where you're already gaining most. Class-balanced sampling plus the elastic warping applied preferentially to ages 1–2 and 8–10 is a natural pairing.

**Probably not worth it:** Mixup at the Ridge stage. Mixing embeddings and targets linearly is a constraint that a linear model already satisfies exactly, so manifold mixup at that layer is close to a no-op. Mixup at the encoder input has the same coherence problem as CutMix, though with an ordinal label the interpolated target is at least meaningful, so it's a defensible one-run check if a reviewer asks. Random erasing is more defensible than CutMix (the label is untouched) but risks occluding the margin; if you try it, mask only the proximal half.

# Two things I suspect matter more than augmentation

**Resolution along the reading axis is the likely bottleneck.** Median crop is 787×333. You pad to 787×787 and resize to 384, so the axis on which increments are counted is downsampled by roughly 2×, and 58% of the canvas is border. The increments most diagnostic of age are the ones packed closest together at the margin, and they are the first casualty of that downsampling. Three concrete moves: (a) SigLIP2's NaFlex checkpoints accept native aspect ratio and variable sequence length, which would remove the padding and the squashing problem outright — worth checking whether one is available at the SO400M scale; (b) DINOv3 uses RoPE, so you can simply run it at 512 or 768 with no position-embedding surgery, and you already note this property in §2.3; (c) tile the wide crop into two or three overlapping square windows along the long axis, embed each at full 384, and concatenate or average. That last one needs no new checkpoint and roughly doubles effective resolution on the axis that matters. I'd run this before any augmentation study, because if it works it changes what augmentation you should be tuning.

**You're discarding labelled data.** The original annotations run to 17 years and you clip to 1–10. Under an ordinal decomposition, an age-13 fish is a positive example at every threshold up to 9 — those specimens still carry supervision for the thresholds you care about. Train with the full label range and collapse predictions ≥10 at evaluation. It costs nothing and adds supervision precisely at the sparse upper thresholds. Separately, if MFRI has unlabelled otolith imagery in archive, continued self-supervised pretraining on it before the LoRA stage is likely to beat any augmentation tweak, given that your curve is still rising and labels are the expensive part.

# One methodological caution

Your seed spread is 1.6 pp, and it's already doing a lot of work in the paper — it's the reason you don't rank encoders and don't claim FFT beats LoRA. Any augmentation comparison is subject to the same floor. A single run showing +1.5 pp from elastic warping is unreadable. I'd budget at least three seeds per augmentation condition and report the mean, and consider evaluating on validation MAE as the primary signal since it's less quantised than accuracy. The strongest framing for the paper, if you want to include this: run augmentation × training-set-size and show that the gain is largest at 586 images. That extends Figure 7 rather than adding an orthogonal ablation, and it's the regime where a fisheries lab starting on a new species actually sits.



Are there any other things we should try to squeeze out more performance besides augmentations?


Plenty. Roughly in order of return-per-effort, and I've split them by whether they need retraining.

# Free — no encoder retraining

**Concatenate two adapted encoders.** You already have four adapted embeddings sitting on disk. SigLIP2 and DINOv3 come from genuinely different pretraining objectives (contrastive vision–language vs self-distillation), so their errors should be partly decorrelated, and concatenating them before the Ridge costs one `np.hstack`. This is the cheapest ensemble available to you and, in linear-probe settings generally, encoder concatenation is one of the more reliable gains going. It also sits comfortably in the paper: you're already arguing that adaptation dominates encoder choice, and "and if you want the last point, take two" is a natural coda rather than a contradiction.

**Use the CORN head you threw away.** You train a linear ordinal head end to end with the adapter and then discard it in favour of a Ridge refit on the pooled embedding. What does that head score on its own? It's a missing baseline, and I'd want to know before anything else — it's task-specific where the pooled embedding is generic, and it might already be ahead. Either way, the nine conditional logits are nine highly informative features: append them to the 1,161-dimensional Ridge input. That's a stacked model with no new training.

**Stop rounding at 0.5.** Ridge predicts continuously and you round to the nearest integer and clip. Those cut-points are set by convention, not fitted. Optimising nine thresholds on the validation split — for macro F1, or for MAE, they'll differ — is a standard and usually worthwhile win on ordinal regression, and it bears directly on your weakest result: age 10 lost F1 because the adapted model predicts it more often and is right slightly less. That's a threshold problem, not a representation problem, and it's fixable at this layer.

**Refit on train + validation.** You hold out 1,466 images (20% of your training data) solely to pick a stopping epoch, and never fold them back in. On a learning curve that's still climbing at 5,860 images, going to 7,326 is a real increment — Figure 7's slope tells you roughly what to expect. Select the epoch count on the split as now, then retrain on everything for that many epochs. Costs one extra run.

**Try intermediate layers.** You read out the final pooled vector. For a fine-grained texture-counting task the last layer is often too semantic; concatenating the pooled outputs of, say, layers 27, 22 and 18 frequently beats the last alone. Pure embedding extraction, no retraining, and it's a two-line change.

# Needs retraining, but principled

**Put LoRA on the patch-embedding projection.** This one follows directly from your own argument in §2.2. You justify CLAHE by saying the patch projection is fixed by pretraining on natural photographs where meaningful structure has far higher local contrast than an otolith increment, and that since the encoder is frozen "the only available lever is to raise the contrast of the banding in the input." But you're no longer freezing the encoder — and yet you leave the patch projection untouched while adapting q, k, v, fc1, fc2. Adapting the tokenizer itself is precisely the intervention your own diagnosis calls for, and it's a small number of parameters. If it works, it also gives you a much more interesting version of the CLAHE story: does the adapted patch projection make CLAHE redundant? That's a paper-worthy result, not just a point of accuracy.

More broadly, §4.4 concedes target modules were never varied. A short sweep (attention only / MLP only / late blocks only / plus patch embed) is the obvious companion to the rank sweep you already have, and DoRA is a cheap drop-in alternative worth one run.

**Ordinal label smoothing.** Your labels are expert readings with real inter-reader variability — you cite a CV of 3.1% for modern sectioned Icelandic cod, and 47–76% per-reader exact agreement in the North Sea exchanges. Training against a hard one-hot target is asserting a certainty the labels don't have. Distributing a little mass onto adjacent ages, with width tied to something like the observed reader disagreement, matches the noise model and typically helps most exactly where you're weakest: the ambiguous older ages. It's also easy to defend to a fisheries reviewer, which is not true of most regularisation tricks.

**Auxiliary heads on fish length and otolith morphometry.** Predicting length and otolith dimensions from the image during fine-tuning is free supervision — you already have those columns — and it pushes the encoder toward exactly the absorption you document in §3.6. I'd expect it to help. But note the cost honestly: it hands the encoder the metadata, which is precisely what §2.4 forbids in order to keep the attribution comparison interpretable. So this is a "how far can we push it" configuration, reported separately from the controlled contrast, not a change to the main pipeline.

**Check the schedule is actually binding.** Thirty epochs, patience five. Which epoch do the runs stop at? If it's early, the run may be underfit rather than converged, and with an unsaturated curve that's worth ruling out before you attribute the ceiling to data volume.

# Data and validity

**Verify the split isn't slide-contaminated.** §4.2 says images were cropped from slides holding 20+ otoliths. You argue there's no leakage because each image is a unique fish, which is true at the fish level — but images from one slide share illumination, mounting, section thickness and camera settings, and if slides were assembled by survey, cohort or landing, then slide identity correlates with age. A model can then read the slide rather than the otolith, and a fish-level stratified split won't catch it. Worth checking whether slide ID is available and, if so, whether a slide-grouped split changes the numbers. If it doesn't, you've closed a hole a reviewer might otherwise open; if it does, you'd much rather know now.

**Both sagittae.** Cod have a pair. If MFRI images both, embedding each and averaging is an ensemble over genuinely independent sectioning and mounting noise, which is a better ensemble than five seeds of the same image.

**The unlabelled archive.** You note labels are the expensive part and the curve is still rising. Continued self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled otolith imagery before the LoRA stage attacks that directly, and MFRI presumably has far more images than readings. Combined with the ages 11–17 you're currently discarding, that's the largest untapped data lever you have.

# One caution on all of this

Most items above are worth somewhere between half a point and two points, against a seed spread of 1.6. Chained together they'll add up to something real, but individually most of them are unmeasurable at your current evaluation resolution, and you'll be tempted to keep whichever ones happened to land above the mean. Pick three or four on prior plausibility, evaluate those on several seeds, and treat the rest as unresolved.

There's also a framing question. The paper's argument is clean — adapt, don't shop for encoders — and it's clean partly because the pipeline stays fixed while one thing varies. Bolting on threshold tuning, stacked heads, encoder concatenation and auxiliary supervision will raise the number and blur the claim. I'd keep the controlled contrast exactly as it stands and add a short "how far can this be pushed" subsection with the best combined configuration, stated as engineering rather than as evidence for the thesis.
